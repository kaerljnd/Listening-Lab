### Academic Talk

**TALK01**

In the 1950s, psychologist Solomon Asch conducted a famous series of experiments on conformity. Participants were asked to look at lines on cards and identify which comparison line matched a standard line. The correct answer was obvious. But each real participant sat with a group of people who were actually working with the researcher. On certain trials, those group members deliberately gave the same incorrect answer.

The result was striking: about three-quarters of participants conformed to the group's incorrect answer at least once. The task itself was easy, so the errors were not caused by difficulty. Instead, the study demonstrated the powerful effect of social pressure on behavior.

Later replications found different levels of conformity. For example, a study with British students in the 1980s found lower rates than Asch's original study. This suggests that conformity is influenced not only by the immediate group but also by cultural and historical context.

**TALK02**

Today we're looking at biohacking, a broad term for attempts to improve physical or mental performance through changes in diet, behavior, technology, or the environment. Some practices are simple, such as intermittent fasting or carefully timing meals. Others involve cognitive enhancers, including substances such as green tea extract, though the evidence for many supplements is mixed.

Sleep is another major area of biohacking. People use wearable devices to track sleep stages, experiment with soundscapes, and adjust room temperature or light exposure. Of these approaches, managing light has some of the strongest scientific support because light affects the body's circadian rhythm. Reducing bright light late at night and getting natural light in the morning can help regulate sleep timing.

The key point is that biohacking ranges from evidence-based lifestyle changes to much more speculative practices. Any claimed benefit should be evaluated carefully rather than accepted simply because a technique is new or popular.

### Academic Talk

**TALK01**

The Renaissance was a pivotal period in European history, spanning roughly the fourteenth to the seventeenth centuries. It was characterized by renewed interest in art, science, and literature and marked a significant departure from the Middle Ages. One defining feature was the revival of classical learning. Scholars and artists looked back to the texts and artistic achievements of ancient Greece and Rome for inspiration. This led to a flourishing of ideas and creativity, evident in the works of figures such as Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and Galileo.

The invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 was another crucial development. It allowed for the mass production of books, making literature and scholarly works accessible to a broader audience. This contributed to increased literacy and the spread of Renaissance ideas.

The Renaissance also saw major advancements in science, particularly astronomy. Nicolaus Copernicus proposed a heliocentric model of the solar system, placing the Sun rather than Earth at the center. Galileo Galilei later used a telescope to observe celestial bodies, providing evidence that supported Copernicus's theory. These discoveries challenged long-held beliefs and laid the foundation for modern astronomy.

Despite political instability in some regions, the Renaissance left a lasting legacy on European society. Its influence can still be seen today in art, science, and education.

**TALK02**

Today let's talk about the ancient Maya civilization and a fascinating development in research on its decline. For years, scholars have debated what happened to this civilization of impressive cities and flourishing culture. Recent research has brought stalagmites into the spotlight. These formations rising from cave floors serve as climate records, preserving evidence of changes in precipitation over thousands of years.

In 2025, scientists analyzed stalagmites from caves near the former Maya heartlands. The data revealed a series of prolonged droughts that coincided with periods of decline in Maya society. The Maya depended heavily on agriculture, with water as a critical resource. When droughts occurred, water scarcity likely placed immense pressure on food production and social structures. Cities could struggle to supply enough water for crops, leading to shortages and unrest.

Climate changes were significant, but they were not the sole cause of Maya decline. Warfare, political instability, and other social factors also played roles. The research shows how changes in natural conditions can interact with social systems and produce broad effects on a civilization.
