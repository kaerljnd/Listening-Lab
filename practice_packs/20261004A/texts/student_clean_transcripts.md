### Academic Talk

**TALK01**

Today I'd like to talk about the color blue in art. Now, we might take blue for granted, but for centuries it was one of the most elusive and expensive pigments an artist could use. In ancient Egypt, artisans crafted blue from lapis lazuli, a brilliant stone mined in what is now Afghanistan. This mineral was so rare and so valuable that it was literally worth its weight in gold. Because of its scarcity, blue was reserved for the most honored subjects in art. In medieval European paintings, important holy figures were often cloaked in deep blue robes. This wasn't just symbolic. It was also a financial statement.

During the Renaissance, oil paints made the mixing and layering of colors easier, including the mixing of lapis lazuli powder with oil. Still, blue remained costly due to its natural source. Then came a turning point in the eighteenth century: synthetic dyes. Prussian blue, which was originally created in Germany quite by accident, was affordable, vibrant, and widely accessible. Suddenly, artists from all walks of life could use blue freely. This democratization of color transformed artistic expression and opened doors for artistic movements like Impressionism, where blue was used freely to evoke atmosphere and emotion.

Even today, blue carries echoes of its luxurious past. It's often linked to ideas of calm, depth, and aspiration. Yet some traditionalists still argue that no synthetic pigment can match the richness and depth of lapis lazuli. The story of blue continues to reflect the evolving relationship between art, innovation, and culture.

**TALK02**

Today we're discussing behavioral economics, a fascinating field that explores how psychological factors influence economic decision-making. Traditionally, economists assumed that individuals made rational choices. However, behavioral economics challenges this notion by demonstrating that people often behave irrationally because of cognitive biases and emotions.

One classic example is the concept of loss aversion. Studies show that individuals tend to prefer avoiding losses rather than acquiring equivalent gains. Since losing 100 euros feels worse than gaining 100 euros feels good, this bias can lead to risk-averse behavior, impacting consumer decisions. The risk of loss seems intolerable even when there is an equal or greater opportunity for gain.

Another factor is present bias, where individuals disproportionately value immediate rewards over future benefits. This can make it hard to save for retirement and can lead people to overspend.

Another behavior that economists examine is confirmation bias, where individuals favor information that confirms their pre-existing beliefs while disregarding contradictory data. Imagine a consumer who strongly believes that Brand X makes the best smartphones. Because of confirmation bias, they might only read favorable reviews about Brand X while ignoring negative ones. Even when presented with objective comparisons showing that another brand has better features or is more reliable, they will downplay that information.

**TALK01**

When we're shopping, we naturally want the experience to be as smooth as possible and to feel we've made good choices. Grocery stores and supermarkets are set up to guide our decisions so we'll be satisfied. It's the result of what's called decision architecture.

Now, our brains are always looking for efficiencies and shortcuts. The world is complex enough, right? Our cognitive tendency is to make our decisions easier, and grocery stores use decision architecture to facilitate those choices, which I guess is manipulative. For me, after a long day at work, I'm up for whatever helps me shop faster. Otherwise, all the options can be overwhelming.

One strategy they use is called defaults. Defaults are basically preselected options. In a store, a default might be an item that's on a shelf at eye level. If you're looking for a jar of pasta sauce, the store wants to promote a certain brand. They'll put it in a place where you'll see it right away and be more likely to pick it up.

There's also framing. It means presenting information that highlights the benefits of an item. Say a store wants to promote certain pancakes. You might see a sign to point out health benefits of those pancakes. Maybe it'll say low sugar. That's good, right? It would not say high in artificial sweeteners, which would make it less appealing. With low sugar, you're more likely to grab that item for your cart.

**TALK02**

Let's start with something you probably touch every day: a piece of glass. Now, you probably know that glass is made from sand, but what you might not know is how similar the process that people use to make glass is to how nature creates a type of rock called obsidian.

Another name for obsidian is volcanic glass. It has a shiny surface and, like glass, it's made primarily of a material called silica, which is found in sand. So how does nature do it? When a volcano erupts, lava flows across the ground. The lava starts to cool. Usually, as the lava temperature drops, it forms crystals, which produce granite rock or basalt rock, depending on the speed of cooling. But occasionally lava cools extremely quickly, so fast that crystals don't have time to form. The silica molecules are instead frozen in place. And this is what creates obsidian rock with its smooth, glassy texture.

Obsidian has been incredibly useful to humans for millennia. In ancient cultures, it was prized for its sharp edges. When obsidian is broken, the fractures are so clean that you can actually make cutting tools out of it that are sharper than modern surgical steel. In fact, obsidian scalpels are occasionally used today in specialized medical procedures.

Geologists study obsidian to better understand volcanic history. Obsidian can act as a clue to help us trace the history of eruptions on Earth and other geological processes. Let's get into that.
