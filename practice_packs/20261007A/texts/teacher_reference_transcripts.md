Since ancient times, humans have studied sound and light. Today we're able to break these forms of energy into smaller components using the Fourier transform, a mathematical technique that decomposes complex signals—like songs or images—into frequencies. Afterward, those components can be manipulated and recombined.

Imagine a photograph of a forest. A Fourier transform can separate broad shapes from fine details, making it possible to filter noise or sharpen features. The same principle works with sound. Audio compression can identify very quiet sounds that are masked by louder ones and remove some of that information, reducing file size while preserving what listeners perceive.

The Fourier transform is also important in medicine. MRI machines collect raw signals that are difficult to interpret directly. Fourier techniques convert those signals into readable images of structures inside the body. This gives doctors useful visual information without requiring invasive surgical procedures.

If you were to take a walk in the Arctic tundra, you'd be walking over permafrost, a soil layer that has remained frozen for thousands of years. As global temperatures rise, however, the stability of this permafrost is compromised. This brings something into play that you might not expect: microbes.

Many microbes have remained dormant in frozen soil. When permafrost thaws, they become active and break down organic material, releasing carbon dioxide and methane. These greenhouse gases trap heat, which can cause more warming and further thawing—a positive feedback loop.

The amount of carbon stored in permafrost is enormous, roughly twice the amount currently in Earth's atmosphere. Scientists therefore study which microbial communities become active and how much gas they produce. Continued monitoring of permafrost regions is essential for improving climate models and developing strategies to address environmental change.

Imagine walking into a store in the United States in the mid-1860s and seeing a small piece of paper attached to an item with its price printed on it. Today that seems ordinary, but at the time it was revolutionary. Before price tags, prices were rarely written down. They existed in the merchant's head and were revealed only during negotiation. Every purchase involved haggling, a process that could be nerve-racking for consumers and often led to unpredictable outcomes.

John Wanamaker, a Philadelphia merchant who opened his first store in 1861 and later created one of the earliest department stores in the United States, is most often credited with popularizing the price tag. His principle of one price ended the chaos of haggling and introduced fairness and transparency to retail. The practice quickly spread across the growing department-store industry, reshaping commerce.

The price tag changed everything. It made the price visible and nonnegotiable, sending a powerful message: this is the price, and it's the same for everyone. That simple innovation built trust among shoppers, who no longer feared being overcharged because of their appearance or lack of bargaining skill. For stores, it streamlined operations. Clerks didn't need to memorize prices or haggle endlessly, and transactions became faster and more consistent. More than a piece of paper, the price tag symbolized a new way of doing business—predictable, standardized, and fair. By the early twentieth century, fixed pricing and price tags were everywhere, shaping the shopping experience we take for granted today.

When you hear the word volcano, you probably picture dramatic eruptions of molten rock—lava. But have you heard of cryovolcanoes? These are volcanoes that exist on extremely cold planets or moons. Instead of erupting molten rock like Earth's volcanoes do, they erupt substances such as water, ammonia, or methane. Essentially, cryovolcanoes spew out ice or icy slush. On frigid worlds, these liquids act like molten rock does in volcanoes on Earth: they are the materials that shift and flow.

Hundreds of cryovolcanoes exist on one of the moons orbiting Saturn, the planet known for the rings around it. The moon's name is Enceladus. The cryovolcanoes on Enceladus eject plumes of water vapor and icy particles into space. Some of this falls back to the surface of Enceladus as snow, which now covers the moon entirely. But some of the ejected material ends up in space and contributes to Saturn's rings by adding particles to them.

It seems that an underground ocean lies beneath the moon's surface. The cryovolcanoes give us insight into this hidden ocean because the material that erupts comes from below the surface. There's even a possibility that the ocean of Enceladus could host the conditions necessary for living organisms, though its environment is far colder than on Earth. So how do we study the erupting materials? Let's look at the tools spacecraft have used to obtain information from these materials and from the moon in general.
