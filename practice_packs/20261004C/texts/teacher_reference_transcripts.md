Previously, we discussed the significance of pottery to ancient cultures, including pottery as an art form and pottery's crucial role in trade. Today we'll focus on the development of pottery over time. One breakthrough was the invention of the potter's wheel around 3500 BCE in Mesopotamia. Before this, pottery was shaped mostly by hand, which was slower and made it harder to create symmetrical forms. The wheel allowed potters to rotate the clay while shaping it, giving them better control over form and thickness.

Another advance was the updraft kiln, which directed heat upward through the chamber. By adjusting the airflow, potters could control how much oxygen reached the fire. This helped them produce pottery with specific colors, like red or black, depending on how the clay reacted to the firing atmosphere. Then, in ancient China, potters developed kilns that could reach very high temperatures, making it possible to create porcelain. Porcelain is hard, thin, and often translucent, but it requires much higher firing temperatures than ordinary pottery.

Let's talk about the French artist Edgar Degas. Even if you don't recognize his name, you've likely seen his paintings of dancers. Degas is known for his depictions of ballet scenes—graceful dancers stretching or practicing. He was born in 1834, and many consider him a key figure in French Impressionism.

Impressionist artists were concerned with using bright colors and the effects of light. They focused on capturing impressions of a scene or a moment. Very often they painted outside and created beautiful landscape paintings. But Degas rejected the label of Impressionist for himself and his art. For one thing, he disliked painting outdoors. He preferred carefully composed scenes and often focused on ordinary people indoors. His ballet dancers are not always presented as idealized performers; he frequently showed them tired, waiting, stretching, or uncomfortable. This emphasis on unpolished everyday moments reflects the influence of Realism.

Degas was also interested in photography. His photographs explored unusual angles, cropping, and lighting, and these experiments influenced the compositions of his paintings and drawings.

When we're shopping, we naturally want the experience to be as smooth as possible and to feel we've made good choices. Grocery stores and supermarkets are set up to guide our decisions so we'll be satisfied. It's the result of what's called decision architecture.

Our brains are always looking for efficiencies and shortcuts. The world is complex enough. Our cognitive tendency is to make decisions easier, and grocery stores use decision architecture to facilitate those choices. One strategy is called defaults. Defaults are basically preselected options. In a store, a default might be an item that's on a shelf at eye level. If a store wants to promote a certain brand of pasta sauce, it may put it where you'll see it right away and be more likely to pick it up.

There's also framing, which means presenting information that highlights the benefits of an item. Say a store wants to promote certain pancakes. You might see a sign pointing out a health benefit, perhaps saying low sugar. It would not say high in artificial sweeteners, which would make the product less appealing. The framing makes you more likely to put the item in your cart.

Let's start with something you probably touch every day: a piece of glass. You probably know that glass is made from sand. What you might not know is how similar the process people use to make glass is to how nature creates a type of rock called obsidian. Another name for obsidian is volcanic glass. It has a shiny surface and, like glass, it's made primarily of silica, a material found in sand.

So how does nature do it? When a volcano erupts, lava flows across the ground. If lava rich in silica cools extremely quickly, crystals don't have time to grow, and the result is obsidian. Slower cooling allows crystals to form, producing different volcanic rocks.

People have used obsidian for thousands of years because it can break into extremely sharp edges. Ancient people made cutting tools and arrowheads from it. Even today, some surgical scalpels use obsidian blades because their edges can be exceptionally fine. Geologists also study obsidian to learn about past volcanic activity and ancient trade networks.
